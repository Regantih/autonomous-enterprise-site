Organization: humans and agents

What changes

Agents absorb tasks, not jobs

A job is a bundle of tasks. Take each job apart, decide what happens to each task, then rebuild the role. Book, Ch 24

Five shifts in the operating model
ShiftBeforeAfter
Who does the workPeople using screensAgents and people together
What gets releasedCode and configurationAlso instructions, skills, models, limits and policies
How cost behavesMostly fixed licensesVariable use on several meters
What failsPrograms and interfacesAlso reasoning, context, drift and cost loops
Who is accountableProcess owner for the processProcess owner for process and agent outcomes

Four classes for every task

  • Absorb. The agent does it; a person audits samples. Typically L3 to L4.
  • Augment. The agent prepares; a person decides. Typically L1 to L2.
  • Retain. A person does it; the agent may inform. Typically L0 to L1.
  • New. Work that did not exist before, such as reviewing the weekly override report.

Common errors

  • Saved hours are not saved people. A 30% saving spread over 85 people frees nobody until roles are rebuilt.
  • Review time is real work. At L2, 2,000 proposals a week at 90 seconds each is 50 hours.
  • Do not hollow out the role. If only hard cases remain, the job exhausts people and the entry path closes.

Book, Ch 24. Figures are illustrative.

Org structure

Agents are owned, not managed

Agents appear on the org chart only as assets of a role. They have no reporting lines, no headcount and no place in spans of control. Book, Ch 24

Each team page lists its people. Under the role that owns them, it lists that role’s agents and their levels. The agent registry, not the HR system, is the agents’ record.

The Autonomy Office is the book’s name for the company’s AI center of excellence. It owns standards and the registry. It does not own business outcomes. Book, Ch 25

The agent business owner matters most

  • Approves the agent’s specification, level, limits and escalation paths.
  • Signs promotions on evidence and reviews overrides at least monthly.
  • Owns the agent’s risk and can stop the agent.
  • Is a business person, not IT. A cross-platform agent gets one owner for the whole outcome.

A vendor survey cited in the book found that only 9.5% of companies name a person formally accountable for agent behavior. Read it as directional. Book, Ch 22 and Ch 24

Spans, layers and what managers do now

  • Managers. Routine supervision shrinks. Reviewing agent output, handling exceptions and coaching grow.
  • Spans. Size them by human decision load, not headcount. AP might widen from 1:10 to 1:14, but only after review load is measured at L2.
  • Reviewer capacity. It sets how many agents a team can supervise. A higher hand-off rate needs more people, not a wider span.
  • Layers. Remove a layer only if it just relayed information the trace store now shows. Keep every layer that holds a decision right or a control signature.

Pay and the junior pipeline

  • Agents are not span. Owning ten agents is not managing ten people. Grade roles by decisions owned and risk carried.
  • Pay for outcomes owned. Owners carry objectives on the agent’s outcome and control record, not its usage.
  • Keep a dual ladder. Context engineers and evaluators can reach senior grades without managing people.
  • Protect the junior pipeline. Keep a fixed graduate intake, an apprenticeship on real exceptions and monthly rotation through manual cases.
  • Fill new roles from inside first. Process knowledge matters more than AI knowledge.

Delegation and decision-making

Agents change how much is decided, not who may decide it

Every step has a level. People set it, on evidence, through a named body. Raising it needs a meeting; lowering it needs one person. Book, Ch 01, Ch 24 and Ch 25

Decision rights by role

  • Process owners set intent and the do-not-automate list.
  • Agent business owners set limits within policy and stop their agents.
  • The autonomy council changes levels.
  • Any on-call engineer stops an agent, without a meeting.
  • The pod lead may route any case to people without approval. Demand for human judgment never waits for a meeting.

Ownership follows the layers. The CFO owns the limits. The CIO owns the harness and the inventory. The COO owns the boundary with physical operations. The CHRO owns role redesign. The chief risk officer and internal audit own the evidence standard. Book, Ch 01

Make approval limits executable

A delegation policy written for people is not a control for an agent. The harness checks each limit before the action, outside the model. Book, Ch 24

Escalation paths

  • Outside its limits, the agent escalates with an evidence pack to a named queue. Book, Ch 01
  • Each step’s target card names the escalation queue and its response time, for example one business day. Book, Ch 01
  • Unanswered approvals go to a supervisor after one business day in the book’s AP example. Book, Ch 05
  • The person who approves an agent’s actions may not change its limits, instructions or tools. Book, Ch 24

One-way and two-way doors

The book does not use the one-way and two-way door framing. It uses reversibility and materiality instead. Irreversible actions, such as payments and external messages, stay at L2. Book, Ch 01 and Ch 22

Levels, limits and bodies are illustrative. Each company sets its own with internal audit.

Decisions with incomplete information

When the evidence is thin, route to a person

Agents rarely have every fact. The book’s answer: calibrate, set the threshold by cost, cite or say what is missing, and escalate when unsure. Book, Ch 03, Ch 05 and Ch 07

Act only above the threshold

Act automatically only when expected gain beats expected loss. The threshold p* = L ÷ (G + L) rises with the amount at stake. Book, Ch 03 The worked table is on Design choices.

A raw score is not a probability until it is calibrated. Use calibrated scores to route, never to approve. Book, Ch 07

Uncertainty bands

Three confidence bands
BandCalibrated confidence (illustrative)Route
Green0.95 or more, and all rails passAct at the step’s permitted level
Amber0.80 to 0.95, or one soft check failsConfirm with the person, or send to a reviewer
RedBelow 0.80, or any hard check failsFall back; do not act

Forecasts carry ranges, not single numbers. A cash buffer is set against the 10th percentile, not the median. Book, Ch 04

Cite, or say what is missing

  • Every factual claim needs a source. The output has a “missing information” field. An agent that cannot cite says what it lacks rather than guess. Book, Ch 05 and Ch 09
  • A decision without the required evidence is “not done”, for example a variance not computed by a tool. Book, Ch 05
  • A summary step that drops a missing-evidence flag is a control defect. Book, Ch 05
  • If input data fails its quality gate, the step is capped at L2. Book, Ch 01

The fallback ladder

  1. Retry with a tighter constraint.
  2. Ask the person a clarifying question. Agents tend to guess rather than ask, so make asking the cheaper path.
  3. Use a safe default, such as a promised call-back by a set time.
  4. Hand off to a person with the full context.
  5. Stop safely: no commit, and an open case.

Book, Ch 07. A fallback that never fired in testing is a hypothesis.

Reversible or irreversible

Materiality and reversibility set the default ceiling. Small, easily reversed actions may reach L4. Irreversible ones stay at L2 or below. Book, Ch 01

An action that commits the company to a third party loses one level for its first 90 days at a new level. Book, Ch 01

Prove it small before you scale

  • Shadow first. The agent runs on live cases and acts on none. Book, Ch 24
  • Pilots end in a decision. Criteria are signed before the build and never changed after. A stop on evidence is a result. Book, Ch 26
  • Enough cases. About 300 clean cases show, with 95% confidence, an error rate below 1%. Below 0.3% needs about 1,000. Book, Ch 26
  • Treat estimates as hypotheses. Each has a baseline, a guardrail metric, a control group and a kill rule. Book, Ch 21
  • Vendor numbers are claims to test. Ask: production or demo, same baseline, review cost included? Book, Ch 21

What the book does not cover

The book has no general method for executive judgment under uncertainty. It treats incomplete information at the level of each step, through thresholds, evidence gates and pilots. It mentions value of information only as a routing rule: use the cheapest engine that clears the threshold. Book, Ch 21
